    Questions about distance and climbing

    Road lengths and stop heights are separate allowances in this puzzle. Count the road you ride and every upward change; these numbers are not bicycle-energy measurements.

    Is the shortest road always available?

    Choose Take the contour. Compare the ridge road with the road through Mill, Bridge and Orchard before riding.

    Read an answer

    The ridge costs four distance and four climbing. The longer road costs eight distance and one climbing. With a climbing allowance of one, the shortest route is not a usable route. The two limits cannot replace each other.

    Does the descent pay back the climb?

    Choose Two hills count twice. Follow the heights along the two-hill road without pressing a destination yet.

    Read an answer

    The road rises from zero to two, falls to zero, then rises to two again. Those upward changes add to four climbing, even though the finish is back at zero. A descent does not refund an earlier rise. The lower towpath fits the separate climbing allowance.

    Can a longer complete delivery need less climbing?

    Compare Deliver and bring back the reply with A gentler complete delivery. Include the ride home, not just the outward delivery.

    Read an answer

    The roof round trip uses eight distance and five climbing. The gentler round trip uses sixteen distance and three climbing. Returning over the roof adds a rise from the cafe’s height of three to the roof’s height of four. The price of going home belongs to the route too.

    Does reversing a loop remove a hill?

    Choose a complete journey with the same roads in either direction. Add only the upward height changes, including the ride home.

    Read an answer

    A closed circuit returns to its starting height, so its total rises equal its total falls. Reversing that identical circuit swaps rises and falls and keeps the same cumulative climbing. A different road can change the price; reversal alone cannot.

    Is a delivery finished at its last letter?

    On a journey whose goal is Post, pause after delivering the final letter. Read the next stop before choosing a road.

    Read an answer

    Not yet. The goal still requires the ride home. Delivered letters do not grant extra distance or climbing, and the allowance already spent stays spent. A route must pay for its whole stated journey.

    Does a taller stop imply a longer road?

    Compare a road length with the heights of its two endpoints before riding it.

    Read an answer

    They are separate authored quantities. Road length spends distance; a positive endpoint height difference spends climbing. Map spacing is a drawing aid, not the road-length formula. Neither allowance is a physical bicycle-energy estimate.

    Does passing a future letter deliver it early?

    Choose Three letters using the school cross-road. Read the requested order before passing School on the way to Orchard.

    Read an answer

    No. This delivery asks for Hill, then Orchard, then School. Passing School before Orchard does not deliver the School letter; you must return when it is the next requested stop. A place on the map and a place in the delivery order are different things.

    Is a road free the second time?

    On an ordered-letter delivery, compare the route list with a road that you have ridden before.

    Read an answer

    No. Every traversal spends that road’s printed distance again. Climbing is also charged for each upward traversal, not once for discovering a hill. An earlier visit grants neither allowance back.

    Does reaching Post before the letters end the ride?

    Begin a delivery whose goal is Post. Read the remaining ordered stops before returning there early.

    Read an answer

    No. Returning to Post finishes only after the requested letters have been delivered in order. An early return is another position on the route; it does not erase the remaining stops or restore distance and climbing.
